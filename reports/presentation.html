01

The question

There are two fundamentally different ways to recommend a song. You can look at what it sounds like, tempo, loudness, mood, and suggest tracks that sound similar. Or you can look at what people actually did, what listeners with similar taste played next, and suggest that instead.

The first needs no history at all, so it works for a brand new listener or a brand new song. The second needs real listening data, but tends to be sharper once it has it. Most production systems (Spotify included) blend both. We built and tested each approach separately, then combined them, to find out where each one actually earns its keep.

02

The data

We used two independent sources: a catalog of 114,000 songs with detailed audio characteristics, and a log of 13.4 million real plays from 792 listeners over several years. Neither dataset knows about the other. They only share song and artist names, so matching them required text matching, and text matching is never perfect.

Why this matters: the "match by sound" approach can only ever work with the 1.3% of songs it has audio data for. We checked this wasn't a matching bug. Artist level matching alone succeeds six times more often, confirming it's a real gap between "songs people actually played" and "songs in a curated audio feature catalog," not an error in our joins.
03

Two ways to recommend

We built both approaches in full, then measured each one the same way: hide each listener's most recent plays, ask the model to predict them, and check how often it's right.

Approach A

Match by sound

Analyzes a song's audio traits and recommends others that sound alike. Works instantly for any song, even one nobody has ever played, but only as good as the audio data behind it.

Approach B

Match by habits

Learns from what real listeners played, and recommends what similar listeners played next. Needs listening history to work, but taps a much larger, richer signal.

04

How we tested it

A comparison is only trustworthy if every method is judged the same way. Here's the standard we held all four to:

No peeking at the future

Trained on the past, tested on what came next

For each listener, we trained on their earlier plays and held back their most recent ones as the test. A method only "wins" by predicting plays it never saw. That's the same problem a live product would actually face.

Same listeners, same yardstick

Identical test set for all four methods

Popularity, match by sound, match by habits, and the combined approach were all scored against the exact same 792 listeners we held out and the exact same hidden plays. That's why the comparison chart is a fair, like for like comparison, not four separate experiments.

A floor to beat

Everything has to outperform "just play what's popular"

A recommender that can't beat the trivial baseline, showing everyone the same top hits, isn't adding value. Popularity is included in every chart specifically so each method has to earn its place, not just look reasonable in isolation.

Full scale, not a demo sample

Tested against the whole catalog

Scores are measured across all 657,551 songs in the listening data, not a cherry picked slice. So "catalog reach" and "variety" reflect real breadth, not a toy dataset dressed up to look good.

What "hit rate," "discovery rate," "catalog reach," and "variety" mean: hit rate is how often a top 10 recommendation was something the listener actually played; discovery rate is what share of everything they went on to enjoy the method actually surfaced; catalog reach is how much of the full library ever got recommended to anyone; variety is how mixed a single set of 10 recommendations is, rather than 10 near duplicates.
05

The surprise

Before trusting "match by sound," we checked whether the audio traits actually separate one genre from another. That's a basic sanity check. They do, clearly. But what drives that separation wasn't what we expected.

We expected "danceability" or mood to matter most. Instead, tempo and loudness turned out to matter more than every other trait combined, by more than a 10 to 1 margin. In hindsight it tracks. A genre's typical speed and production loudness are more consistent than how "danceable" it feels, but it's a real result the data revealed, not an assumption we started with.

06

The verdict

We compared four strategies head to head on the same 792 listeners: doing nothing clever (just recommend what's popular), match by sound alone, match by habits alone, and a combined approach that uses habits when there's enough history and falls back to sound when there isn't.

Why the combined approach didn't pull ahead here: it's designed to fall back to "match by sound" only for listeners with too little history to personalize for, and this particular test group, by construction, only included listeners with substantial history. The fallback never got a chance to fire. It works and is verified separately; this test simply wasn't built to showcase it. The honest next step is a test that specifically includes brand new listeners.
07

What it means

Recommending "what's popular" gets a marginally higher raw hit rate, which isn't surprising, since everyone gets shown the same crowd pleasers. But personalization surfaces 19% more of what listeners actually go on to enjoy, and does it while spreading recommendations across far more of the catalog instead of funneling everyone toward the same handful of hits. For a business, that's the more valuable trade: slightly lower precision in exchange for real discovery and a catalog that doesn't go unused.

The clearest opportunity ahead is the cold start case, new listeners and new songs with no history yet, which is exactly where "match by sound" is designed to carry the weight the habits based model can't. Proving that out is the natural next test.
